## Prevalence

Prevalence: approximately 10% of patients in the United States report a penicillin allergy, but fewer than 1% of the population is truly allergic. About 80% of patients with an IgE-mediated penicillin allergy lose their sensitivity after 10 years. Many recorded "allergies" reflect non-allergic adverse effects (for example GI upset), childhood viral rashes, or family history.

## Consequences of unverified labels

Consequences of unverified labels: patients labeled penicillin-allergic are more likely to receive broader-spectrum or less effective alternatives, with higher rates of resistant infections, Clostridioides difficile infection and treatment failure. Evaluation and de-labeling are recommended where appropriate.

## Risk stratification

Risk stratification (2022 practice parameter): history determines risk. Low-risk histories include remote (more than 5 to 10 years ago) benign cutaneous reactions, unknown reactions, or symptoms not consistent with allergy; the parameter supports direct oral amoxicillin challenge without prior skin testing for low-risk patients in an appropriate setting. High-risk histories include anaphylaxis, recent severe reactions, or severe cutaneous adverse reactions (Stevens-Johnson syndrome, toxic epidermal necrolysis, DRESS); penicillins should be avoided in patients with a history of severe cutaneous adverse reactions.

## Relevance to pharmacist review

Relevance to pharmacist review: before dispensing a penicillin-class drug to a patient with a recorded allergy, clarify the reaction type, timing and whether the patient has since tolerated a penicillin or cephalosporin. The decision belongs to the prescriber and pharmacist, not to an automated system.
